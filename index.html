In Memoriam

Shinn-Der Chang

1929 2023

A scholar from coastal Fujian, war-displaced inland and across an ocean. An engineer who built towers and heavy-water plants. A grandfather who tended a garden in Ontario for twenty years.

I have been blessed. I have no regrets. My life has been beautiful. Shinn-Der Chang, April 2023
China
I.
1929 · Fuzhou, Fujian

A restaurant on Nan Jie 南街

“My grandmother was a gardener. She was blind from the age of forty, and she raised me.” Shinn-Der, 2022

He was born in 1929 in Fuzhou, on the southeastern coast of China. His father owned the largest restaurant on the city's main shopping street — Nan Xiong on Nan Jie — a place that stretched more than eight storefronts deep behind its facade. As a small child, he sat in the accountant's office and counted money.

He was raised primarily by his grandmother, a woman who had gone blind at forty yet still kept a vegetable garden. The family drew strength from her mother's side — a large Fuzhou household that quietly supported them through lean seasons.

II.
1937 · Nanping

Four hundred kilometres inland 内迁

“Then the second war broke out. The school moved 400 km away from Fuzhou, in Nanping city. There were Japanese bombings. Four classes dropped to one — seventy-five percent were gone.” From the 2018 oral history

When Japan invaded in 1937, the family was displaced. Schools moved inland, and his followed — four hundred kilometres from the coast, into the mountain city of Nanping. The bombings found them there too. Attendance thinned until four classrooms held what had once taken one.

He finished high school in industrial studies — a pragmatic choice in a collapsing country. The restaurant in Fuzhou had lost its profitability during the occupation. The world he had grown up in was quietly ending.

III.
1948 · Taipei

A load of textiles, and a love 布与爱

“He asked my brother and myself to take the load of product and move to Taiwan to sell. So I was there — and I met my love.” Shinn-Der, Christmas 2017

In 1948, his father asked him and his brother to carry a shipment of textile bolts across the Taiwan Strait and sell them. They went. While they were on the island, the Communists took the Mainland. He did not return.

He took work as a technician at a wine-bottle factory in Taipei — chemistry training put to use — and lived frugally. One day he wandered into the Bureau of Liquor and Tobacco next door, where a young comptroller was at her books. Her name was Rae. He was twenty-three.

He was admitted to National Taiwan University — thirty thousand applicants for one thousand seats — and studied Chemical Engineering. A job at a glass-bottle factory paid his way. In his second year, he and Rae married.

IV.
1959 · Kaohsiung

Climbing the towers 炼油厂

“At the refinery I climbed the towers. That is how you learn a process — from the inside out.”

After an eighteen-month posting as a military reserve officer near Kaohsiung, and the birth of his first son, Nathan, in Taipei, he joined the Kaohsiung Petroleum Refinery as a process engineer. It was prestigious work, and it was hands-on. He climbed the distillation towers. He learned, tray by tray, how crude oil yields its fractions. The knowledge would matter later, in another country, in a colder sea.

V.
1963 · Ottawa

$145 a month 留学

“$145 a month. Tuition was about $90, so I had $60. I had $30 to pay for the rent, and I needed $20 for food. I sent about $30 home to support these three.” On the PhD stipend, 1963–67

In 1963, through a connection of Mrs. Wong's — a Dr. Liu — he was offered a PhD scholarship at the University of Ottawa. He went ahead alone. Rae stayed in Taiwan with Nathan and, by then, with Margaret. The arithmetic of his stipend was exact and pitiless: $145 a month, and a list of mouths to feed on two continents.

For four years the couple wrote letters across the ocean. The family still has them. He finished his PhD in 1968.

In 1967, Rae arrived in Canada. She came in time for his graduation.

VI.
1969 · Port Hawkesbury, Cape Breton

Two inches, not four-and-a-quarter 重水塔

“I had so much practice on the computer, I figured out I could convert the process into a program. Finally they said they should try. When they did, they could produce 320 tons of deuterium.”

Stan Davies had read his distillation papers. In 1969 he was hired at the GE heavy-water plant — starting salary $14,500. In 1972 the family moved to Cape Breton.

The plant would not reach capacity. For two years he modelled the tower, tray by tray, in Fortran. The computer told him the trays were too far apart — two inches was the correct plate height. The plant had been built with four-and-a-quarter.

They rebuilt on his analysis. Production jumped to 90%+. Output reached 320 tons of deuterium a year.

VII.
1995 — 2023 · Toronto & the Cottage

Twenty winters in the garden 晚年

“Having gone through twenty winters and summers, / flowers planted and vegetables grown — diligence fills the garden.” Guoqing Li, “Chang's Cottage,” 2025

After the heavy-water years came the quieter ones. The family moved to Toronto. A cottage in Ontario became the family's hearth. He gardened; he canoed; he slept through long afternoons. The grandchildren came and went, broke things, were patiently repaired for.

In April 2023 a diagnosis came: primary liver cancer, weeks not months. There was no pain. “Life goes fast,” he told his daughter Margaret, “but I have been blessed.”

He died in June 2023, surrounded by family. He was ninety-three.

Jonathan Yue
Grandson · Quiet Support

On the stipend, and the mouths it fed across two continents.

A poignant illustration of this was in 1963, when he moved to Canada and lived on his PhD stipend while attending the University of Ottawa. Of the one hundred and forty-five dollars he earned each month, the majority went back to China and Taiwan — to Grandma Chang and the kids, to Grandma's mother, to Grandpa's parents.

That was Grandpa. The quiet arithmetic of how much he needed, and how much he could give away.

Jonathan, July 2023
Michael Yue
Grandson · Quiet Courage

Three leavings, each one of them a quiet act of courage.

As a young man, he moved to — and stayed in — Taiwan. As a young adult, he emigrated to Canada and raised his family there. As an older adult, he showed courage investing in high-leverage real estate, backed up by his own sweat equity. Yet somehow I had known little to nothing about any of these until the 2010s.

Grandpa's courage never announced itself. It simply did the thing, and waited for the rest of us to notice — if we ever did.

Michael, July 2023
Daniel Yue
Grandson · Quiet Patience

On the walls we broke, and the man who always came to fix them.

We used to roughhouse around our Maryland home, and on occasion would break the plaster walls when Michael would — too enthusiastically — push one of us through them. Fortunately for us, this seemed to happen shortly before visits from Grandpa, who would always come, patiently take out his plaster and putty, and fix the walls — if only for them to be broken again before the next visit.

I think now that the walls were a small lesson in a much larger patience. The broken walls of a life — mine, ours — have always been quietly fixed by someone. For a long stretch, that someone was him.

Daniel, July 2023
Margaret Chang
Daughter

Written in April 2023, after the diagnosis — an update the family still re-reads.

Dad understands, and has said more than once: “Life goes fast… but I have been blessed.” He has no pain, despite how far the cancer has travelled. We consider this a grace. He is home, with Mom, and the palliative team are in place.

Mom has been more resilient than I thought. She told me: “He has been the best husband and father. We need to do right by him.”

Margaret, 15 April 2023

種花種菜勤滿園
景美木秀秋時爽
出入往來靠行船

Having gone through twenty winters and summers, / flowers planted and vegetables grown — diligence fills the garden. / The view is beautiful, the trees stand out; autumn is refreshing. / Coming and going depend on boats. / Memories rise in every place and stir the heart, / warm kinship fills the home, a gentle, constant glow. / The long river runs on, its waters never part; / children grown, a new generation set to grow.

Guoqing Li, August 2025
